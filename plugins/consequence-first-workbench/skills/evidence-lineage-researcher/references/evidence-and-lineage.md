# Evidence and lineage

## Evidence record

Keep the minimum structure needed to audit the claim:

| Field | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Claim | What exact proposition needs support? |
| Source | Which inspected source bears on it? |
| Locator | Where in the source is the relevant material? |
| Relation | Supports, defines, exemplifies, limits, contradicts, or contextualizes? |
| Scope | How much of the claim can the evidence carry? |
| Independence | Is this source independent or repeating another source? |
| Currentness | Which date, version, or edition applies? |
| Confidence | Established, well-supported, plausible, disputed, or unresolved? |
| Notes | What reasoning connects source to claim? |

Use an existing argument map, citation system, database, or project ledger when available. Do not create another permanent ledger solely for the research pass.

## Evidence-chain rules

- Each item should make a distinct evidentiary contribution.
- Place evidence near the claim it supports.
- Distinguish evidence from an implication derived after the claim is accepted.
- Treat an example as evidence only for the scope it can establish.
- Expose missing premises that no citation can supply.
- Record contradictory evidence rather than selecting only agreeable sources.
- Avoid duplicate nodes created by minor wording changes or repeated passages.

When one source contributes to several claims, specify the different passage or function for each use. If the function is identical, consolidate the evidence rather than multiplying it.

## Lineage map

For each proposed predecessor or adjacent thinker, record:

- the relevant work and date;
- the exact conceptual move shared with the current argument;
- the important difference in premises, mechanism, scope, or conclusion;
- evidence of direct influence, indirect transmission, shared source, or independent convergence;
- the appropriate strength of attribution.

Use these attribution levels accurately:

- **direct influence** — documented reading, citation, correspondence, transmission, or acknowledgment;
- **lineage** — a defensible chain of concepts or sources connects the positions;
- **precedent** — the earlier work makes a materially similar move without demonstrated transmission;
- **parallel** — the positions converge from different premises or contexts;
- **analogy** — the comparison illuminates but does not establish a shared position or method.

Do not equate similarity with influence. Do not erase difference to make the lineage look stronger.

## Institutional position and synthesis

Separate four layers:

1. what a primary text states;
2. how a historical community or institution interpreted it;
3. what later scholarship argues;
4. what the current author synthesizes from those materials.

Use institution-level language only for positions established by representative sources. Present novel synthesis as the author’s analysis, even when it develops material found within the field.

Acknowledging precedent should sharpen the current argument. State what was inherited, what was independently reached, and what is being revised or extended.

## Citation audit

Before delivery, check:

- every direct quotation against its source;
- every link against the claim beside it;
- names, dates, titles, editions, and translations;
- citation reuse and duplicate evidence nodes;
- claims broader than their sources;
- statements of consensus, novelty, priority, or influence;
- current claims against current authoritative material;
- clear labels for inference, reconstruction, and uncertainty.

Remove citations that decorate the prose without supporting its actual claim. Add argument where the gap is logical rather than documentary.
