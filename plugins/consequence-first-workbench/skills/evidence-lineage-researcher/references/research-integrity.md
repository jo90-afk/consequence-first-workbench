# Research integrity

## Define the evidence task

Classify each target:

- **stable factual claim** — a durable fact with a clear authoritative source;
- **current claim** — a fact, role, price, law, standard, product, schedule, or best practice that may have changed;
- **historical claim** — an assertion about events, chronology, reception, or institutional development;
- **textual claim** — wording, translation, manuscript, edition, or interpretation of a source;
- **scholarly claim** — a result, method, dispute, or field position;
- **technical claim** — behavior defined by documentation, standards, source code, or reproducible results;
- **interpretive claim** — an argued reading that requires both evidence and reasoning;
- **lineage claim** — priority, influence, similarity, inheritance, or divergence among thinkers or systems.

Do not let one source type stand in for another. A dictionary can establish an etymology but rarely an institution’s historical intention. An official policy or standards document establishes the institution’s stated position but not how every member understands it.

## Source hierarchy

Choose the strongest available source for the claim.

### Texts and history

1. the primary text in the requested edition or translation;
2. critical editions, official archives, or institutional documents;
3. peer-reviewed or expert scholarship with direct engagement with the source;
4. reputable reference works for orientation;
5. tertiary summaries only for discovery.

### Scholarship and mathematics

1. the paper, preprint, dataset, or formal result itself;
2. official journal, conference, or repository record;
3. later papers that reproduce, extend, or dispute it;
4. expert expositions for explanation, not proof of priority.

### Software and standards

1. current official documentation, specification, or source repository;
2. release notes and migration guidance for version changes;
3. reproducible behavior in the relevant version;
4. maintained expert guidance when official material is incomplete.

### Companies, markets, and current practice

1. filings, official data, product documentation, or first-party announcements;
2. regulators, standards bodies, and recognized industry research;
3. independent reporting or analysis for comparison and critique;
4. vendor claims only within the scope they can establish.

Use multiple domains or independent sources when incentives, controversy, or claim breadth make triangulation useful.

## Search and reading method

1. Search the exact claim, names, key terms, and likely primary source.
2. Open the candidate source and read enough context to understand its actual claim.
3. Locate dates, versions, editions, definitions, methods, and limitations.
4. Follow citations backward for origin and forward for reception or correction.
5. Compare publication date with the event date for current or news-like claims.
6. Retain direct source links and locators while researching; do not try to reconstruct them later.

Search results, snippets, AI summaries, and citation indexes are discovery tools. They are not evidence until the underlying source is inspected.

## Quotations and translations

- Verify every direct quotation against the source actually cited.
- Record edition, translation, version, section, page, paragraph, line, or timestamp as appropriate.
- Do not silently modernize, harmonize, or combine translations.
- If the user specifies a translation or phrasing standard, use it consistently and mark quotations drawn from another version.
- Keep paraphrase outside quotation marks and preserve the source’s degree of certainty.
- When a requested full passage exceeds permissible quotation length, provide a short verified excerpt and a faithful summary.

## Currentness

Treat laws, standards, product behavior, roles, prices, schedules, and best practices as time-sensitive. Verify them at the time of the request and name the applicable version or date when useful.

Do not call a practice “best” merely because several recent articles repeat it. Identify the outcome, constraints, evidence, and domain in which the recommendation performs well.

## Links and access

Link directly to the page that supports the claim. Prefer stable source locations. If a search index or scholar portal blocks direct access, use the publisher, DOI resolver, official archive, author-hosted copy, standards page, or another lawful canonical source.

Do not cite a search-results page when the source page is available.
