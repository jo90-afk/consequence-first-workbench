# Claim functions

Classify a sentence or passage by the work it actually performs. Grammatical form and rhetorical emphasis are unreliable guides.

## Definition

A definition establishes how a term or model will operate.

> Derived state is information computed from an accepted fact and the dependencies that fact governs.

This can define the argument’s object without proving how common the object is. If the definition claims to report established usage rather than stipulate usage for the piece, it may also require evidence.

Test: Can later claims be evaluated only after this meaning is fixed?

## Contestable claim

A claim asks the reader to accept a proposition about the world, a text, a history, or a model.

> Teams often update an initiating fact without rebuilding the dependent state that fact controls.

Test: Could an informed reader coherently disagree? If so, identify the necessary evidence or argument.

## Evidence

Evidence increases the warrant for a claim. It may be empirical data, a source, an observed case, textual detail, testimony, or reasoning from accepted premises.

Evidence should sit close to the claim it supports. Do not treat a second assertion, a vivid analogy, or a quotation without interpretation as evidence by default.

Test: What becomes more credible because this material is present?

## Implication

An implication develops the state of the argument after a claim is provisionally accepted.

> If dependent state remains stale, a correct input can leave the system behaviorally wrong.

An implication may be necessary, probable, or merely possible. Name that strength accurately.

Test: Does this follow from established premises, or does it introduce another premise that must be defended?

## Synthesis

A synthesis combines multiple established propositions into a new claim.

> A change is complete only when every affected surface represents the new state.

This conclusion may require a definition of derived state, evidence about where stale state persists, and an account of which surfaces depend on the initiating fact. It can carry genuine surprisal even when each premise is familiar.

Test: Could the reader recover this proposition from any single earlier claim? If not, the combination may add real information.

## Qualification and boundary

A qualification adjusts strength, scope, or conditions. A boundary identifies what the argument does not reach.

> Propagating a correction does not require replacing every matching string; quoted and historical uses can remain valid.

Use boundaries to prevent overclaiming, not to perform ritual balance. A concentration is different from a boundary: discovering that a problem is especially dense in one chapter or subsystem does not establish that it exists only there.

Test: Does this alter the proposition’s applicability, or merely sound cautious?

## Repetition

Repetition changes wording while leaving the proposition unchanged.

Common disguises include:

- replacing concrete terms with abstractions;
- converting a claim into a rhetorical question;
- announcing that a distinction is important;
- adding a metaphor that illustrates but does not develop;
- restating the thesis at every section boundary;
- treating the author’s changed feeling as a changed argument.

Test: If the reader fully understood the earlier passage, what new proposition, evidence, consequence, or boundary would this one supply? If the answer is none, it repeats.

## Genuine surprisal

Genuine surprisal occurs when the argument produces information that was not already available by paraphrase. It may take the form of:

- an unforeseen consequence;
- a synthesis across claims or domains;
- a boundary that materially revises scope;
- evidence that reverses the expected relation;
- a definition that reorganizes familiar observations;
- an application that exposes a latent property of the model.

Surprisal need not be shocking. It must be new. Announcing that a distinction “changes how we think” supplies no surprisal; defining what the distinction establishes may.
