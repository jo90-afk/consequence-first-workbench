# Architecture audit

Use this process for an existing essay, chapter, manuscript, talk, or strategy document.

## 1. Build a proposition inventory

Record the minimum useful fields:

| Field | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Claim | What proposition is actually asserted? |
| Function | Does it define, assert, support, imply, synthesize, qualify, bound, or repeat? |
| Dependency | What must already be understood or accepted? |
| Support | What gives the reader reason to accept it? |
| Scope | Local, general, universal, historical, interpretive, or conditional? |
| Novelty | What information does it add beyond prior claims? |
| Destination | Which later claims depend on it? |

Collapse sentences that express the same proposition. Do not mistake frequency for architectural importance.

## 2. Trace dependency

- Identify claims introduced before their terms or warrants exist.
- Identify definitions that arrive after the argument has already depended on them.
- Identify evidence separated so far from its claim that its function becomes unclear.
- Identify conclusions that require an unstated premise.
- Identify sections whose order follows topic association rather than logical need.

An opening may state an unearned thesis as a promise. Mark it as such and ensure the body eventually earns it.

## 3. Find repetition and stalled movement

For each paragraph, ask what becomes available to the argument afterward. Flag a paragraph when the answer is only “the prior claim has been said again.”

Distinguish useful recurrence from repetition. A recurring claim can acquire a new function when a later context tests it, limits it, applies it, or reveals a consequence. Merely changing vocabulary does not.

## 4. Audit surprisal

Remove rhetorical formulation mentally and paraphrase the proposition. Then ask:

1. Was this proposition already explicit?
2. Is it a necessary inference the reader was never asked to draw?
3. Does it combine premises that have not yet been connected?
4. Does it materially change scope, stakes, mechanism, or application?
5. Is the apparent novelty only the author reporting a realization?

Score surprisal only after this test. Dense language can hide a static argument; plain language can carry a large conceptual gain.

## 5. Audit evidence

- Match the kind of evidence to the kind of claim.
- Verify that examples illustrate no more than they can establish.
- Narrow claims whose breadth exceeds their support.
- Distinguish a missing citation from a missing argument; one cannot automatically repair the other.
- Preserve original analysis as the author’s analysis rather than falsely attributing it to an institution or field.

## 6. Audit boundaries

Separate three questions:

- Where is the problem concentrated?
- Across what material does the problem recur?
- Where does the evidence permit the analysis to stop?

Concentration identifies density. Boundary identifies scope. Do not confuse them when recommending where an edit begins or ends.

## 7. Rebuild movement

Choose the smallest structural change that restores conceptual movement:

- move a definition before its dependents;
- combine repeated proof;
- place evidence beside its claim;
- convert a repeated section into consequences or application;
- add a missing premise or narrow the conclusion;
- reserve synthesis until its components exist;
- revise headings to expose the gain.

When reporting the audit, name both the structural problem and its consequence for the reader. When revising prose, remove the visible scaffold after it has done its work.
