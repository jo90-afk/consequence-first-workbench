# Argument Architecture

Build arguments by dependency and consequence rather than by rhetorical sequence. A reader should be able to tell what has been established, what supports it, what follows from it, and where it stops.

## Load the relevant guidance

- Read [references/claim-functions.md](references/claim-functions.md) when classifying claims, building an outline, or drafting a new argument.
- Read [references/architecture-audit.md](references/architecture-audit.md) when diagnosing or restructuring an existing argument.
- Read both for a full manuscript or essay architecture pass.

## Core method

1. Recover the governing thesis in its strongest defensible form. Do not replace it with a more familiar argument.
2. Inventory the substantive propositions. Classify what each one does: define, assert, support, qualify, synthesize, imply, bound, or repeat.
3. Map dependencies. A claim must appear after the propositions needed to understand or support it, unless the opening deliberately states a thesis that the piece will later earn.
4. Separate evidence from implication. Evidence gives a reader reason to accept a claim; an implication develops what becomes true if the claim is accepted.
5. Remove repetitive proof. Once a proposition is established to the standard the piece requires, use later space to develop consequences, boundaries, or genuinely new evidence.
6. Test for genuine surprisal. Complexity of formulation, a new metaphor, or an announcement of significance does not create a new proposition.
7. Match evidence to breadth. A universal claim requires broader support or a narrower formulation than a local observation.
8. Make headings state the argumentative gain of their sections.
9. Preserve unresolved tensions. Do not manufacture closure to make the outline look complete.

## Governing distinctions

- A **definition** fixes what a term means in the argument. It may organize everything that follows without functioning as evidence for itself.
- A **claim** asks the reader to accept something contestable.
- **Evidence** supports acceptance of a claim.
- An **implication** follows from a claim already granted.
- A **synthesis** combines established claims into a proposition that none supplied alone.
- A **boundary** states where a claim ceases to apply or what it does not establish.
- **Repetition** restates an existing proposition without adding support, scope, consequence, or qualification.
- **Genuine surprisal** is new conceptual information, not difficulty, emphasis, or authorial discovery.

## Output discipline

Match the artifact to the request. Use a claim ledger or dependency map when the user asks for analysis, planning, or an argument map. When the user asks for prose, use the architecture internally and deliver prose rather than a visible scaffold.

Do not force every argument into the same outline. Dependency determines order; genre determines how much of that structure should remain visible.
